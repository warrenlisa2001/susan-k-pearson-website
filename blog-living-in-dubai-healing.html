Living in Dubai and the Hidden Cost of Always Being On

Why so many high-achieving expats arrive in Susan's practice exhausted — and what the body is actually asking for.

By Susan K. Pearson  ·  May 2026  ·  9 min read

Dubai attracts a particular kind of person. Ambitious, capable, driven. Someone who left a comfortable life in another country to build something here — a career, a business, a family, a future. Someone who is very good at performing.

And most of the time, that performance is extraordinary. Dubai rewards it. The city runs on momentum, on networking, on being visible and available and on. There is always a dinner, a deal, a school run, a Zoom call with someone in a different time zone. There is always something that needs doing, and most of the people I see are very, very good at doing it.

But underneath that performance — and I see this so consistently it no longer surprises me — there is often a body that is profoundly depleted. Not just tired. Something deeper. A nervous system that has been in a low-grade state of activation for so long it has forgotten what rest actually feels like.

The Particular Stress of Expat Life

I want to name something that doesn't get talked about enough: expat life is genuinely hard on the nervous system, in ways that are specific and often invisible.

You are far from the relationships that formed you — family, old friends, the places that hold your history. Even if you chose to leave and are glad you did, there is a cost to that distance that shows up in the body. The nervous system is a social organ. It regulates itself in relationship with others. When the people who know you most deeply are ten time zones away, something in you is quietly working harder to feel safe.

At the same time, Dubai asks you to build a new social world from scratch — while also performing professionally, managing logistics, perhaps raising children in a country that is not your own. This is not impossible. People do it every day. But it is a lot, and the body keeps score.

"The body doesn't care that you completed the deal, managed the school run and made it to the dinner. It cares whether it felt safe today. Most of the time, in modern Dubai life, it didn't fully."

What Depletion Actually Looks Like

The clients who come to me in this state rarely describe themselves as stressed. Stress implies an obvious cause — a crisis, a deadline, a conflict. What they describe is more like a persistent flatness. A sense of going through the motions with the volume turned down. Sleep that doesn't restore. A body that aches in vague, unlocatable ways. An irritability that flares faster than it used to. A creeping feeling that something important is being lost, even as everything looks fine on the surface.

Sometimes they say: I don't know what I need. I just know I need something.

That something, in almost every case, is the same: their body needs to be given permission to stop. To be held. To receive, rather than give or perform or manage.

Why the Body Won't Just Switch Off

One of the things I explain to clients — particularly those who say they've tried meditation and found they can't sit still — is that the nervous system cannot simply be told to relax. It needs to be shown that it is safe. And safety is a felt experience in the body, not a thought in the mind.

When someone lies down for a session and I begin working, something happens in the first few minutes that no amount of breathing exercises can replicate. The body receives the unmistakeable signal: you are not alone. Someone is here. You can put it down for a moment.

The quality of that contact — patient, attuned, without agenda — is itself a form of medicine. Not metaphorically. Neurobiologically. The co-regulatory effect of a calm, present nervous system on a dysregulated one has been documented in research on mother-infant bonding, on therapeutic relationships, on the healing power of touch.

What I See in Dubai, Specifically

Dubai is a young city in terms of its population. Most people here are not from here. Which means most people here carry, at some level, the weight of having chosen this — of having justified that choice, of having made it work, of keeping the story coherent. There is a pressure, sometimes spoken and often unspoken, to be thriving.

And many people are thriving, genuinely. But thriving is not the same as flourishing. Thriving can be a performance. Flourishing happens when the body is in it — when you feel things fully, rest deeply, connect authentically, and have enough space inside yourself to actually inhabit your life rather than just manage it.

"Most of my clients are not broken. They are just very, very far from themselves. The work is not fixing — it is returning."

What the Work Offers

In a session, we are not trying to solve anything. We are not analysing, strategising or optimising. We are doing the opposite: creating conditions for the body to complete what it has been quietly accumulating — the stress responses that never fully discharged, the grief that got filed away, the exhaustion that was too inconvenient to feel.

What often happens is that people leave a session feeling lighter in a way they cannot fully explain. Not because something was fixed. Because something was released. Because for ninety minutes, they didn't have to be anything for anyone. They were just a body being cared for. And that, for many people in Dubai, is genuinely radical.

If any of this resonates — if you recognise that persistent flatness, that vague ache of depletion, that sense of going through the motions — I'd like to hear from you. Not to sell you a session, but because what you're describing matters, and there is something that can help.
